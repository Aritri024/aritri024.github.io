Overview

Building the experimental system from concept through validation

The objective of this work was to create a repeatable experimental platform for studying high-temperature combustion and thermal-fluid behavior while retaining enough modularity to change hardware and operating conditions without rebuilding the complete system.

My work extended beyond operating the experiment. I developed the mechanical hardware in SolidWorks, converted design concepts into manufacturing-ready components, integrated gas-delivery and instrumentation hardware, commissioned the test platform, and used experimental measurements to evaluate the resulting system.

Engineering problem

The test platform needed to support controlled high-temperature operation while providing optical access for diagnostics and accommodating interchangeable burner hardware. Mechanical geometry, material selection, alignment, gas delivery, instrumentation, and experimental repeatability therefore had to be considered as one integrated system.

My role

I worked across mechanical design, component definition, prototype assembly, instrumentation, process controls, experimental planning, troubleshooting, diagnostic integration, and data analysis. This allowed design decisions to be evaluated directly through hardware testing rather than treating design and experimentation as separate activities.

Mechanical Engineering

How I translated the design into manufacturable hardware

The mechanical design had to balance experimental flexibility with practical fabrication and assembly. I therefore treated manufacturability, tolerances, interfaces, material compatibility, and future hardware changes as part of the design process rather than addressing them only after the geometry was complete.

CAD & architecture
Developed SolidWorks models for the burner and modular test hardware, allowing component geometry and interfaces to be evaluated before physical fabrication.
Tolerance definition
Applied GD&T, tolerance stack-up, and fit/clearance analysis to define mechanical relationships between components and support reliable assembly.
Manufacturability
Used DFM/DFA principles, engineering drawings, and BOM development to convert experimental concepts into hardware that could be machined, assembled, serviced, and modified.
Materials
Selected materials suitable for the high-temperature experimental environment and ammonia-compatible process hardware, with prototype components operating near 1000°C.
Modularity
Designed the experimental platform so hardware could be changed and evaluated without requiring a complete rebuild of the test system, supporting iterative experimental development.
System Integration

Connecting mechanical hardware, gas delivery, sensors, and controls

Once the mechanical hardware was assembled, the project became a system-integration problem. I commissioned a multi-stream ammonia/methane/air delivery system using mass flow controllers and integrated temperature and pressure sensing with NI cDAQ hardware.

LabVIEW was used for flow-controller setpoints and automated data logging, enabling synchronized and repeatable testing. Bringing these systems together required checking flow delivery, sensor behavior, controls, mechanical interfaces, and data acquisition as a complete experimental system rather than troubleshooting each subsystem independently.

During commissioning and testing, I used root-cause analysis and FMEA principles to isolate hardware, flow-control, instrumentation, and diagnostic issues and restore repeatable operation.

Test & Validation

Using experiments to evaluate the hardware

The completed platform was used for structured experimental testing rather than a single demonstration point. I planned a four-variable Design of Experiments and combined system measurements with several optical and emissions-diagnostic techniques.

  • FTIR spectroscopy was used to evaluate gaseous emissions, including NOx and ammonia.
  • Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) was used to characterize flow behavior and provide quantitative velocity-field information.
  • Chemiluminescence imaging provided optical information on flame and reaction-zone behavior.
  • Computed tomography measurements extended the optical diagnostics to three-dimensional reconstruction.
  • Python and MATLAB were used to automate processing, statistical analysis, visualization, and experimental-data workflows.
Data & Analysis

Turning experimental measurements into engineering information

The diagnostic systems generated substantially more data than could be processed efficiently by hand. I automated Python and MATLAB workflows for image processing, calibration, statistical analysis, and visualization, with typical optical datasets containing approximately 200–300 images per test condition.

Automating the workflow made it practical to compare repeated operating conditions and evaluate system behavior using consistent processing methods. The goal was not simply to generate plots, but to convert experimental measurements into information that could be used to assess the hardware and guide engineering decisions.

Experimental platform and performance validation

The completed system produced repeatable process data and supported engineering evaluation in collaboration with CenterPoint Energy and the University of Minnesota Duluth.
